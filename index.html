The ride

Two problems that look like one

A bike disco is a parade with a soundtrack. Somewhere between twenty and a hundred cyclists roll through the city in a line, and the whole thing only works if everyone hears the same song at the same moment.

That single requirement hides two completely separate engineering problems, and the crew has already solved one of them.

Problem one is getting sound out. A line of a hundred bikes is long — call it two hundred metres nose to tail. One speaker at the front is useless to the back half. You need several speakers spread down the line, all playing the same audio, tightly in step. Solved: they switched from Bluetooth to SKAA, and the cutting out stopped.

Problem two is deciding what plays. Two to five people need to skip a track, kill the volume for a junction, or queue something for the bridge — from wherever they happen to be in the line. That one is still open, and it is the reason for this page.

The two problems feel related because they both sound like "wireless". They aren't. Confusing them is exactly what sent the last attempt off a cliff.

The music travels by radio.
The instructions travel by internet.
They never touch.

The problem they already fixed

The wall at eight metres

Worth understanding, because it explains why the rig is built the way it is. Bluetooth was never designed to cross a moving parade.

At the front

Right beside the phone, everything is fine. This is why it always works in the pub car park when you're testing it.

Eight metres back

Roughly four bike-lengths. The link starts stuttering — a fraction of a second of silence, then a chunk of the chorus missing. On a moving parade this is constant.

Twenty metres back

Gone. And twenty metres is nothing: a hundred cyclists in formation stretch ten times that far. Bluetooth was never in the running.

The fix they found

One transmitter, four speakers, locked together

SKAA is a 2.4 GHz audio protocol that solves a different problem from Bluetooth. Bluetooth pairs one source to one sink and does its best. SKAA takes one transmitter and feeds up to four receivers at once, holding them in step with each other to within forty microseconds — about a thousand times tighter than the human ear can detect.

The other half of the win is that its delay is fixed. Bluetooth's latency wanders between roughly 120 and 200 milliseconds depending on the handset, the codec and the interference. SKAA always takes the same 36 milliseconds. A predictable delay is one you can ignore; a wandering one is what makes two speakers sound like a flanger.

Worth knowing before you scale

Four receivers is a hard ceiling per transmitter. A hundred-bike parade may want more than four speakers spread down the line — that needs additional transmitters, all fed the same audio from the same phone through a splitter. More on that below.

The problem that's left

The phone is at the front. You are not.

With SKAA the sound is handled. But the phone driving it is cable-tied to one bike at the head of the parade, and the people who need to change what's playing are scattered through the line.

The needs are ordinary: skip a track that isn't landing, drop the volume approaching a garda checkpoint or a residential street, queue the big one for the final stretch. What isn't ordinary is doing it from ninety metres back, while moving, in the dark.

There's also a hard constraint that shapes everything below: everyone has their own Spotify account, and nobody wants to surrender theirs for the night.

So the crew tried Spotify Jam. And it fell apart in a way that's worth looking at closely, because the failure has been misdiagnosed — and the wrong diagnosis leads straight to building a network you don't need.

Press play

Everyone is together. This is the bit that gets tested, and it always looks fine.

Thirty seconds in

Phone B is running through a Bluetooth speaker, which adds its own delay that nothing compensates for. It's now a fraction of a beat late — enough to hear as a flam on the snare.

Two minutes in

The weak-signal phone has been rebuffering. The old handset decodes a touch slower. They are now most of a bar apart. Users report gaps of three to seven seconds in the wild.

The diagnosis

This was never a connectivity problem. It's a counting problem: four devices were making sound, and four independent clocks will always come apart. No network fixes that — and note what it doesn't prove. Jam isn't broken. Get the count down to one and the same feature works perfectly.

The fix isn't a better network.
It's one device making sound.

The idea that doesn't work

Putting everyone on one network

Here is the reasonable instinct. Remote control works flawlessly at home on Wi-Fi. There's no Wi-Fi on the street. So: give everyone a private network that follows them around — a Tailscale tailnet — and you've recreated the living room.

It's a genuinely good instinct, and it's wrong for two independent reasons. Either one on its own would sink it.

First: Spotify's remote control was never using your Wi-Fi. Every way of controlling someone else's playback — a Jam, or two devices sharing one account — routes through Spotify's own servers. Different phones, different carriers, opposite ends of the country: it works, and no local network is involved at any point. There's a long-running thread on Spotify's community forum from people reporting cross-network control as a security hole, which is about the strongest confirmation you could ask for.

Second: a tailnet couldn't fake a local network anyway. Local discovery — the thing that makes a speaker appear on your home Wi-Fi — runs on multicast. Tailscale routes unicast traffic only; multicast and mDNS don't cross it, and it's a long-standing open request on their tracker. The one job you'd be installing it for is the one job it can't do.

The verdict on Tailscale

Don't install it. It adds a second app, a second login and a second thing that can be "off" on the night — in exchange for solving a problem you don't have. Keep it in your back pocket for something else; it's a fine tool aimed at the wrong target here.

The account question

Nobody has to give up their Spotify

There are exactly two ways to change what's playing on somebody else's phone, and choosing between them is the whole design decision.

Spotify Connect is same-account only. The device list you see is your account's device list, and no tier or setting changes that. Two people on separate accounts, standing in the same room, cannot control each other's playback at all. The only route is everyone logging out and back in as one shared account.

Spotify Jam is the cross-account route — the one actually built for this. Everyone stays on their own account. The host starts a Jam, shares a link, and guests can play, pause, skip and add to the queue.

With one catch that is very likely why the first attempt disappointed: guest controls are off by default. Until the host switches them on, guests can add to the queue and nothing else — which feels precisely like a feature that doesn't work.

Join in person, at the meeting point

Guests joining a Jam remotely need Premium of their own. Guests joining in person — phones held close together for a moment with Bluetooth on — do not. Get everyone joined before you roll out and it costs nothing, whatever plan they're on.

The answer

Two planes that never touch

Here is the whole design in one idea. The parade runs on two separate systems, and almost every mistake comes from mixing them up.

The audio plane is radio. One phone, one cable, one SKAA transmitter, four speakers. No internet, no Wi-Fi, no accounts. If the playlist is downloaded, this half keeps working in a tunnel.

The control plane is the internet. Admin phones — on their own accounts — send instructions into a Jam, which Spotify's servers pass down to the disco phone. Tiny messages: a skip is a few hundred bytes, not a song.

The disco phone is the single point where the two meet. It is the only device on the entire parade permitted to make a sound. Everyone else holds a remote control.

Setting it up

Five minutes at the meeting point

The disco phone gets prepared at home. The admins do nothing at all until everyone is together at the start of the ride, and then it takes about a minute between them.

Before you leave the house

The disco phone

The one on the sound bike, cabled to the transmitter. It hosts the Jam.

  1. Whoever owns this phone needs Premium — their own everyday account is fine. Nobody else changes anything.
  2. Download the playlist for offline. The most valuable line on this page: a coverage blackspot then cannot stop the music.
  3. Plug into the SKAA transmitter. Phone volume around 80% and leave it — do the real volume on the transmitter.
  4. Do Not Disturb on. A phone call will duck your music in front of a hundred people.
  5. Mobile data on. Battery pack attached.
  6. Start the playlist, then start the Jam.
  7. Jam details → Guest settings → turn on guest controls. Miss this and your admins can queue tracks but never skip one.
At the meeting point, once

The admin phones

Two to five people. Nothing to install, nothing to log out of.

  1. Stay on your own account. Nothing about your Spotify changes, tonight or afterwards.
  2. Join the Jam: hold your phone near the disco phone with Bluetooth on, or scan its QR code. Doing it here, in person, is what makes it free on any plan.
  3. Turn your own volume to zero. Your phone streams along silently — that's normal, and nobody hears it.
  4. Never plug your phone into a speaker, or pair it with one. That is the single thing that breaks the night.
  5. Skip, pause and queue from your own Spotify app, from anywhere in the line.
The host has to stay put

If the host leaves the Jam or closes Spotify, the Jam ends for everyone. The music keeps playing on the disco phone, but every remote goes dead until somebody restarts the Jam and reshares the link. Leave the disco phone's Spotify open and untouched.

The golden rule

Make no noise

In a Jam every phone in the session streams the same track — that's how it keeps everyone's queue and buttons in step. Yours included. As long as your phone is silent and plugged into nothing, that is completely invisible.

The second a phone starts driving a speaker, there are two sound systems on two clocks, and you are back to the night everybody remembers. One picture to teach:

SILENT · PLUGGED INTO NOTHINGVolume at zero, in the Jam

You are a remote control. Skip, pause and queue all land on the rig. Your own phone makes no sound and nobody knows it's streaming.

CONNECTED TO A SPEAKERYour phone is making noise

You are now a second sound system — on your own clock, drifting further from the rig every second. This is exactly what went wrong last time. Disconnect it.

Your phone is a remote,
not a speaker.

How far it stretches

Put the sound bike in the middle

This is the constraint most likely to bite on a big night, and it's about geometry rather than software.

SKAA reaches about 45 metres. A hundred cyclists riding two abreast at a sensible spacing is somewhere around 150–200 metres of road. So the transmitter cannot cover the whole parade from the front — riding at the head, it serves the first quarter and abandons everyone behind.

Move the sound bike to the middle and the same 45 metres works in both directions: roughly 90 metres of covered parade for free, just by changing where one bike rides.

If you want more than four speakers, you can add transmitters — up to five SKAA cells can run alongside each other — but every transmitter has to be fed the same audio from the same phone, through a splitter, on the same bike. That gets you up to twenty speakers; it does not get you extra distance.

What you can't do is put a second phone on a second sound bike further down the line. Two phones means two clocks, and you are back to the Jam problem — this time with real speakers on the street.

The rule of thumb

One phone. One position in the line, in the middle. Speakers spread within 45 metres of it. If the parade is longer than that, the tail hears the crowd instead — which is a formation decision, not a technology one.

If Jam turns out flaky

The bulletproof fallback

Jam is the right first choice because nobody touches their account. But it has moving parts: a link to reshare each event, a guest-controls toggle that can end up off, and a host who has to stay in the session.

The alternative removes every one of those, at a single cost. Make one shared Bike Disco Premium account, put the disco phone on it, and have the admins sign into it too. Now everyone is on one account, so Spotify Connect applies: open the app, tap the Connect icon, choose DISCO PHONE, and you're a remote. No invite links, no per-event setup, nothing to toggle.

The cost is real. Signing into the Bike Disco account means signing out of your own for the night. And it brings one new failure mode: a single account plays on one device at a time, so an admin who presses play without selecting DISCO PHONE first drags the music into their pocket and the street goes quiet. Recovery takes five seconds — select DISCO PHONE, press play — but it has to be taught.

Three settings make it work, and the first is the one everybody misses:

  • Settings → Apps and devices → turn off "Show local devices only". Left on, the disco phone stays invisible precisely because it isn't on your Wi-Fi.
  • iPhone: iOS Settings → Spotify → Local Network → on.
  • Name the disco phone DISCO PHONE, in capitals — not "iPhone (3)". Everyone has to spot it in a hurry.

Connect also drops idle devices after roughly ten minutes; wake the disco phone and it reappears in the list.

Worth five minutes of testing

You may be able to have both. Leave the Spotify app on your own account, and open open.spotify.com in your phone's browser signed into the Bike Disco account, using the web player purely as a remote. If the Connect picker shows DISCO PHONE there, nobody logs out of anything. Mobile browsers are inconsistent about this — test it before you rely on it.

If somebody fancies building something

A web page with three enormous buttons

There's a third route that beats both of the above on sheer ease of use, at the cost of an afternoon from whoever is technical.

Spotify's Web API can drive playback on any device the account holder has authorised. Authorise it once against the disco phone's account, keep the token on a small server, and put three huge buttons on a web page: SKIP, PAUSE, QUIETER.

The admins then need no Spotify account, no app, no login and no invite link. They bookmark one URL and hit a button wearing gloves, in the dark, at speed. Nothing else on this page comes close for that.

The catches: the controlled account needs Premium; the token can't live in a public web page, so this needs a small backend rather than plain static hosting; and anybody holding the link can press the buttons, so keep it obscure and be ready to rotate it.

The decision

Use a Jam.
Skip the
tailnet.

A Jam does exactly what you wanted Tailscale to do — across separate accounts, over the mobile network, with no shared Wi-Fi anywhere in the picture. Switch on guest controls, keep one device making sound, and there is nothing left for a tailnet to solve.

What to actually do

  • One phone, cabled to the SKAA transmitter, Premium, playlist downloaded. It hosts the Jam.
  • Turn on guest controls. This is the step that decides whether the whole thing works.
  • Everyone joins in person at the meeting point, keeping their own accounts, free on any plan.
  • Every admin sets their own volume to zero and plugs into nothing.
  • Ride the sound bike in the middle of the line, not the front.
  • Teach one rule: your phone is a remote, not a speaker.

Worth a dry run

  • Three phones, three different networks, Wi-Fi off. Host a Jam, enable guest controls, and confirm a guest can genuinely skip a track. Ten minutes in a park settles the entire question.
  • Check the music keeps playing on the host phone when a guest's phone loses signal or leaves.
  • Confirm four speakers is enough coverage for the size of parade you're planning.
